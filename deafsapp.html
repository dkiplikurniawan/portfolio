DEAFSAPP

1. Problem

At SLB Melati Aisyah, a special-needs school in Medan, Deaf and hard-of-hearing students communicate in BISINDO, while many hearing teachers, family members and peers are still learning it. The school had few practical BISINDO tools that run on an ordinary smartphone without extra hardware such as sensor gloves.

DEAFSAPP set out to support two narrow tasks for this community:

  • Learning: practise BISINDO vocabulary through lessons and quizzes.
  • Assisted communication: recognise a limited set of signs from the camera and show them as text and speech, and show spoken words as text for Deaf users.

It does not translate BISINDO in general and does not replace interpreters. The thesis system recognised a fixed vocabulary of isolated signs, which is a much smaller problem than understanding continuous, natural signing.

Why these choices

  • Flutter gives one codebase for Android and iOS, matching the goal of running on ordinary smartphones without extra hardware.
  • Landmarks instead of raw pixels: hand and pose landmarks reduce each frame to a small set of coordinates, which needs far less training data and computation than an image model. This is the general rationale for the approach; the thesis-stage implementation details could not be re-inspected.
  • GetX keeps each feature (onboarding, speech, quiz) in its own module with a controller, binding and view.

3. Dataset and experimental design

Reported (thesis) The thesis-stage classifier covered 26 dynamic BISINDO signs.

The following details are not currently documented and are not claimed here:

  • how many recorded sequences there were per sign, and how many signers recorded them;
  • how the data was split into training and test sets, and whether the same signers appeared in both;
  • preprocessing (landmark normalisation, sequence length, augmentation) and the classifier architecture.

These matter: if the same people appear in training and test data, accuracy measures how well the model recognises those signers, not new ones. Without the split, the reported figure cannot be read as signer-independent performance.

Experiments needed to make this result defensible

  1. Signer-independent evaluation: hold out entire signers (leave-one-signer-out) rather than random sequences.
  2. Per-class precision, recall and F1 plus a confusion matrix, to find which signs are confused.
  3. A simple baseline (e.g. nearest-neighbour on averaged landmarks) to show what the sequence model adds.
  4. Robustness checks across lighting, backgrounds, distance and phone cameras.

5. Usability evaluation

Reported (thesis) A field test at SLB Melati Aisyah with 17 students and 8 teachers produced a usability score of 76.5.

The instrument still needs confirming from the thesis. If it was the System Usability Scale (SUS), 76.5 is a score out of 100 (not a percentage), which is above the commonly cited SUS average of 68. If it was the share of positive questionnaire responses, it should be reported as a percentage instead. Results from 25 participants at one school indicate acceptance in that setting; they do not generalise to all Deaf users.

6. Privacy and data flow

What can be verified from the public V1 code:

  • No camera access in V1, so no video is captured or stored.
  • Speech-to-text uses the device's built-in speech service through the speech_to_text plugin. Depending on the phone, that service may send audio to the platform provider (for example Google or Apple) for recognition. The app itself does not store transcripts.
  • No back end or account storage. The sign-in form is UI only.
  • One external request: the quiz fetches questions from opentdb.com.

Whether the thesis-stage recognition ran fully on the device cannot be verified without its source, so this page makes no on-device or privacy-preserving claim for it. A rebuilt version should document its data flow explicitly.

8. Limitations

Current limitations

  • Small vocabulary: 26 isolated signs; no continuous signing, grammar, or facial expressions, which carry meaning in BISINDO.
  • Unverified generalisation: no documented signer-independent test; sensitivity to lighting, camera quality and hand position is untested.
  • Lost artefacts: the recognition model, AR code and raw evaluation data were not preserved, so results cannot be re-run.
  • Small, single-site usability sample (25 participants, one school); instrument to be confirmed.
  • Uncertainty not shown to users: a low-confidence prediction is presented like a confident one.

Future work (proposed, not done)

  • Rebuild recognition with a documented, versioned dataset and a signer-independent evaluation protocol.
  • Show confidence and let users correct wrong predictions.
  • Add BISINDO-specific lesson and quiz content, replacing the trivia placeholder.
  • Co-design and evaluate the next version with Deaf users and teachers, reporting the full instrument and results.

9. What this project taught me

  • A headline accuracy number is only as useful as the documentation of how it was measured.
  • Code, models and evaluation data need version control from the start; losing them made the original results impossible to reproduce.
  • Accessibility work has to define its scope honestly: a fixed set of recognised signs is useful for learning, but it is not sign-language translation.
